1. Buying, also called "going long"

The one everyone already understands.

You buy a share for 100. Later you sell it. If you sell above 100 you made money; below 100 you lost money. The desk calls this a long, and the price you paid is the base.

LongBuy first, sell laterProfits when the price goes up. Loses when it goes down. The blue LONG tag on the screens means this.
BaseThe price you paidEvery line is measured from it. On the chart it is the white dotted line with the "fill" mark.
FillThe moment the order went throughYou ask to buy; the broker finds a seller; the trade "fills". The fill price becomes the base.
Real example from your lab · AMZN, 10 NovBought 3 shares at 269.77 (the base). Later the price was 272.18, so each share was up 2.41, about +0.9%, and the 3 shares together were up about $7.
Bought3 × 269.77 = $809Price later272.18Gain so far+0.89% · about +$7

2. Short selling, "going short"

The one that trips everybody up at first.

A short is the same trade done in the opposite order. You sell first shares you borrow from the broker, and you must buy them back later to return them. If you buy back cheaper than you sold, you keep the difference. If you have to buy back dearer, you lose.

ShortSell first, buy back laterProfits when the price goes down. Loses when it goes up. The violet SHORT tag means this.
Why it existsTo make money from a fallWithout shorts the desk could only wait when it thinks a price will drop. With them it can act on that too.
The catchUp is bad for a shortA chart that looks nice (price going up) is a losing chart for a short. Always check the tag first.
Real example from your lab · GLD, 10 NovSold short 2 shares at 368.03. Later bought them back at 369.22. It paid 1.19 more per share than it had received, so it lost.
Sold short2 × 368.03 = $736Bought back369.22Result−0.43% after costs · −$3.14
This is the trade you asked about: "bought low, sold high" was the wrong way round, because it was a short. (It also closed by mistake at a restart; the price never reached its max-loss line.)

3. Reading a candle

One candle is one minute on the lab's charts.

Each candle shows four prices for its minute: where it opened, the highest and lowest it reached, and where it closed. The thick part runs from open to close; the thin wick marks the high and the low.

Green candleClosed higher than it openedThe price went up during that minute.
Red candleClosed lower than it openedThe price went down during that minute.
O H L COpen, High, Low, CloseThe readout under the chart shows these four for the minute under the time runner.
VolumeThe small bars at the bottomHow many shares changed hands that minute. Tall bars mean a busy minute.

The lines watch the high and the low of each minute, not only the close. If the low of a minute touched the max-loss line, the line counts as hit even if the minute closed above it.

4. The three lines on a long

Your rule, drawn. Steps of 0.3% from the base.

The moment a trade fills, the desk draws a base at the fill price and a max-loss line below it. Then it measures the price in steps (0.3% of the base each) going up. One step up and the max-loss line jumps to the base: from then on the trade cannot lose. Every further step raises the min-profit line one step, so it always sits one step below the best price seen. The trade closes when the price comes back to the line.

Max-loss lineThe most you can loseStarts below the base (0.5% to 1.5% below, the predictor chooses). If the price falls to it, the trade is closed. For stocks the same line also rests at the broker as a stop order, so it holds even if the desk is down.
First stepMax-loss line → baseOne step (0.3%) above the base and the max-loss line moves up to the base. Worst case from now on: a scratch, about −0.1% after costs.
Min-profit lineThe profit you have locked inStarts rising from the second step on, one step at a time, always one step below the best price. If the price comes back to it, the trade is closed with that profit.
RatchetA line that only moves one wayThe lines go up, never down. That is what "ratchet" means on the screens: the line was raised another step.
Real example from your lab · AMZN, 10 Nov
19:44Price one step up (270.58): max-loss line moved to the base 269.7719:48Price two steps up (271.39): min-profit line raised to 270.5819:58Three steps up (272.20): min-profit line raised to 271.39
If the price now falls back to 271.39 the trade closes with +0.6% locked in (about +$4.80 on $809, before costs). If it keeps rising, the line keeps following.

5. The same lines on a short

Everything mirrored: "up" means down.

On a short the max-loss line sits above the base, because a rising price is the danger. The steps count downward: one step below the base and the max-loss line drops to the base; every further step down pushes the min-profit line down, one step above the lowest price seen. The red zone is on top and the green zone below, which is what you saw on GLD and NFLX.

Short, max-loss lineAbove the baseIf the price rises to it, the trade is closed with a loss.
Short, min-profit lineBelow the baseMoves down behind a falling price. The trade closes when the price bounces back up to it.
NFLX, 10 NovWhy nothing movedA short at 62.61 that only went up. Not a single step in its favour, so the lines stayed put, and the max-loss line at 62.99 closed it at −0.72%.

6. The ways a trade ends

Every closed position names one of these on the screen.

Word on the screenWhat happenedUsually means
reached the maximum loss lineThe price went against the trade all the way to the max-loss line.A loss, the biggest one allowed.
came back to the baseOne step up had moved the max-loss line to the base, then the price fell back to it.A scratch: about −0.1% after costs.
came back to the min-profit lineThe price had risen several steps, then came back to the locked-in line.A profit, the locked-in amount.
ran out of timeThe predictor gave the idea a time limit (the horizon, often 3 hours) and it passed.Small gain or loss, wherever the price was.
closed before the bellStocks and funds are sold 10 minutes before the New York close so nothing is held overnight.Wherever the price was at 20:50 UTC.
the broker's stop firedThe stop order resting at the broker closed it before the desk's own order did.Same as reaching the max-loss line (or the base, once moved).
flattenedThe desk found a position at the broker that no line owned and closed it.A clean-up, not a prediction.

7. Percent, money and costs

Why "+0.5%" on the screen is a few dollars.

NotionalWhat the position was worth at the fillShares × price. 2 GLD at 368.03 = $736. Each lab position is about 1% of the account, so about $1,000.
PercentChange in price from the baseFrom 368.03 to 369.22 is +0.32%. For a short that is a loss of 0.32%, because it had to buy back dearer.
Round tripBuying and selling onceEvery trade costs a little on the way in and the way out: about 0.1% for stocks and funds, 0.5% for crypto (its fees are higher).
After costsWhat is really leftGLD: −0.32% from the price, minus the 0.1% round trip, is −0.43%. On $736 that is −$3.14. The screens always show numbers after costs.
Paper accountPretend money, real pricesThe lab trades a practice account at the broker. The fills and prices are real; the dollars are not. That is how the rule is tested before any real money.
Why 0.3% stepsBigger than the costs, smaller than a day's moveA step must be worth more than a round trip (0.1%), and small enough that a normal day crosses several of them. Crypto steps are five times bigger because its costs are.

8. Who does what

The agents, in the order a trade passes through them.

SentinelThe eye on every instrumentLooks at all 50 instruments every minute. No thinking of its own: when something changes (a surge, a flip of mood, fresh news) it raises a trigger.
PredictorThe one that makes the callReads everything known about the instrument and answers: long, short or none, how sure it is (confidence), and where the lines go. Uses the Claude model.
SkepticArgues against the callLooks for what is wrong with it. "Blocking" stops the trade; "concern" is written down beside it.
Risk officerChecks the rulesIs the market open, is the size allowed, is there already a position? Every order passes here, and a closing order always gets through.
Execution and the brokerSends the order, gets the fillAlpaca is the broker. It fills the order and holds the stop order for stocks and funds.
Lines engineHolds the position by your ruleFrom the fill to the exit: watches every minute, moves the lines, sends the closing order, writes the score.
Trigger"Look at this one now"Examples: "surge: strength 47 to 59", "side flip: bear to bull", "review: bull 57 for 45 minutes".
ConfidenceThe predictor's own oddsIts honest guess that the trade reaches its target before the max-loss line. 0.5 is a coin toss. The desk trades at 0.55 or more.
LeanThe side it would pick anywayEven when it says "no trade" it names the side it leans to, so the report can check it later on paper.

9. Right and wrong

How the right/wrong report keeps score.

RightClosed with a gain after costsEven a small one. +0.10% counts as right.
WrongClosed with a loss after costsIncluding a scratch at the base (−0.1%) and a close at the bell that was slightly down.
On paperChecked without tradingWhen the predictor named a side but the desk did not trade, the same lines are run over the real prices that followed, so you still learn whether it would have been right.
The bar for the predictor55% right over 60 callsBelow that, the predictions (not the lines) need to change. The report counts toward this every day.

Word list

WordIn plain words
LongBought first. Wins if the price rises.
ShortSold first (borrowed shares), bought back later. Wins if the price falls.
BaseThe fill price. All lines are measured from it.
Max-loss lineThe price at which the trade is closed to cap the loss. Below the base on a long, above it on a short. Moves to the base after the first step.
Min-profit lineThe profit already locked in. Rises one step at a time behind the price (falls, on a short). The trade closes when the price comes back to it.
Step0.3% of the base for stocks and funds (five times that for crypto). The unit the lines move in.
RatchetA move of the min-profit line by one step. Only ever in the trade's favour.
TargetHow far the predictor expected the price to go. Used to judge its confidence; not a line.
HorizonThe time limit the predictor gave the idea. The trade closes when it passes.
FillThe order going through at the broker. The fill price becomes the base.
Stop orderAn order left at the broker that sells (or buys back) by itself at the max-loss line, so the position is protected even if the desk is down.
NotionalShares × price at the fill. The money in the position.
Round tripThe cost of one buy and one sell: about 0.1% for stocks and funds, 0.5% for crypto.
After costsThe result with the round trip taken off. Every number on the screens is after costs.
Paper accountA practice account: real prices and fills, pretend money.
TriggerThe sentinel saying "look at this instrument now".
CallThe predictor's answer to a trigger: long, short or none, with confidence and lines.
ConfidenceThe predictor's own odds of reaching its target before the max-loss line. The desk trades at 0.55 or more.
LeanThe side the predictor would pick even when it does not trade. Graded on paper.
PassA call of "none": the predictor saw no side with better than even odds.
On paperA call that was not traded but is scored as if it had been, on the real prices that followed.
Right / WrongGain or loss after costs when the trade closed.
Flat / flattenedHolding nothing in an instrument. "Flattened": the desk closed a position no line owned.
Market hoursNew York stocks and funds: 14:30 to 21:00 UTC, Monday to Friday. Crypto: all day, every day.
Candle, O H L COne minute of prices: open, high, low, close. Green closed up, red closed down.
Time runnerThe slider under the chart. Drag it to read the price and all three lines at any minute.
